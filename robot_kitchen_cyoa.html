Make me a sandwich.

You're at a terminal wired to a robot. It will do anything you type — exactly, literally, with zero common sense. There's bread, a jar of peanut butter, a knife on the counter.

Where do you want to start?

It does what you say.
Never what you mean.

Fast, tireless, perfectly obedient. One flaw: no common sense, no benefit of the doubt, no "you know what I mean." It is the most literal thing you'll ever work with.

// hold that thought — it's the whole course.

you@kitchen:~$ put the peanut butter on the bread

It picks up the jar. It places the jar. On the bread. Lid still closed.

✓ FOLLOWED EXACTLY

Not broken. Not stubborn. It did what you said.

The robot was right.
Your instruction was wrong.

WHAT YOU SAID

Put the peanut butter on the bread.

Two nouns, one position. Unambiguous to a machine.

WHAT YOU MEANT

Open the jar, take a knife, scoop a spreadable amount, distribute it across one face of one slice.

~12 hidden steps, all assumed.

The robot obeys your words, not your wishes. That gap is where every bug lives.

Giving instructions
is hard.

Not because you're bad at it. Because language leans on shared understanding — and a machine shares none of yours.

You get specific:

you@kitchen:~$ open the jar and spread the
  peanut butter

Better! But you never said with what. No knife in the instructions — so it improvises with the only tool it has. Itself.

✕ NEW FAILURE UNLOCKED

Every order hid things
you never said.

Humans fill gaps automatically. The robot exposes every gap you left:

  • A jar must be opened before its contents exist to you
  • "Spread" needs a tool — a knife, held a certain way
  • "On the bread" means one face of one slice
  • A sandwich implies a second slice to close it
  • "Some" peanut butter is a quantity it can't guess

Unstated assumptions = preconditions. Naming them is half the job.

Break it down till
nothing is assumed.

Split one fuzzy command into atomic steps a literal machine can't misread.

That diamond is a decision. That loop is iteration. You're writing control flow.

Order is part of the meaning.

Same steps, wrong sequence, worse sandwich. The robot won't reorder them for you.

Spreading onto a closed sandwich. Opening a jar you no longer need.

A decomposed, ordered, assumption-free spec:

// sandwich.spec
get(bread, 2)
open(jar)
grip(knife)
while not covered(slice[0]):
  scoop(knife, jar)
  spread(knife, slice[0])
stack(slice[1], onto=slice[0])
serve()
✓ SANDWICH ACHIEVED

The failures all have names.

You'll use these every term — same four, in every language:

Broke the grammar — bad order, missing piece
→
syntax error
Grammar fine, the action's impossible — spread, no tool
→
semantic error
It ran, then fell over mid-task
→
runtime error
It worked perfectly — and made the wrong thing
→
logic error

You just wrote
a program.

The robot was never the point. It's every literal machine you'll ever instruct — a compiler, an interpreter, or the AI you'll prompt later. They all make sandwiches exactly the way you ask.

Decomposition, sequence, decisions, loops, naming your assumptions — that's the whole foundation, and you found it by bullying a robot about lunch.
